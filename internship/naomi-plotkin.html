Position overview

The intern will train inside a retail real estate brokerage and advisory firm. masonre represents landlords and brands in New York, Miami, and Los Angeles. The work is one lifecycle: understand a building, decide who belongs in it, find that brand, and communicate the opportunity.

Part II · The book

Who we are.

masonre is a brokerage and advisory firm. We work in New York, Miami, and Los Angeles. We lead the growth of trophy retail: the buildings, and the brands that make those buildings matter.

The name is masonry. Brick, mortar, craft, a foundation that holds. The standard we use on ourselves is the same one we will use on you: quality over quantity. Fewer things, done properly. Partnership ahead of the transaction. Hustle, and very little ego.

We do two jobs that look different and are the same skill.

For a landlord, we decide who the building is for, merchandise the space, tell that story, and lease it. Sometimes we are in the work before the landlord buys. Sometimes the retail has been dark for years and the job is to change what the street thinks the building is.

For a brand, we learn the customer, pick the streets, and negotiate the stores. A good assignment is a rollout, not a single lucky corner.

Read those two ideas in the brand book until you can say them with examples. Start here:

  • 1140 Third Avenue, Upper East Side. We broke a large retail floor into smaller spaces, put a fitness studio in the lower level off a new 67th Street entrance, and leased Alo, Credo, Bondi Sushi, Madison Reed, Solidcore, and Aesop. Solidcore’s number one store in the chain.
  • 75 Kenmare, Nolita. New retail under a residential building. We merchandised it, leased it, and the retail condo sold.
  • 101 William, Financial District. Retail vacant for nearly a decade. The plan was one best-in-class gym for the offices above and the neighborhood. Club Studio, 36,000 square feet, their first New York gym.
  • Oaklyn, Oakland Park. The brochure was written before the shovel. It had to show a campus, not a list of square feet. Pure Barre, Bondi, Pause, and a set of local and national operators followed.
  • Alo. A lease on the Upper East Side when the brand had eight stores. Then a South Florida rollout, and the purchase of 100 Lincoln Road for a flagship, an office, and a private gym.
  • Boggi, Peserico, Bondi, SunLife, Reforming Pilates. Tenant work. Different customers, same muscle: where they should be, and in what order.

You are allowed to know these. They are the firm’s public record. If you retell one, retell it accurately, and do not add numbers you did not read.

Part II · The path

Eight steps, in order.

You will touch all eight during the year. The order is the order you should learn them. October is step 1, the brand book, and the script. Do not jump to filming, and do not jump to outreach, before you can say what the firm does.

  1. 01

    Real estate vocabulary

    The glossary at the back is the list. Read it on the subway, between classes, whenever you would otherwise scroll. In October, mark every term you cannot explain out loud. Ask Jeff the ones that still feel slippery after you have tried. By February the words should show up in your Slack notes without you reaching for this book.

  2. 02

    Landlord strategy

    A landlord assignment starts before a lease draft. Someone has to decide who the building is for. At 1140 Third that meant smaller stores and a fitness use downstairs. At 101 William it meant one gym strong enough to wake up a retail box that had sat empty. At Oaklyn the brochure had to describe a campus before the building existed.

    With Jeff, pick one building from the brand book and one block you can actually walk. Write half a page: who lives or works here, what is already on the street, what is missing, and what you would refuse even if they could pay the rent. That is strategy. Square footage is not.

  3. 03

    Discovering brands

    Agency work runs on a list of the right names, kept current. Some of those names are for a building we represent. Some are brands we represent, looking for their next street.

    Brandon is the person who will ruin a lazy list. A logo you like is not a reason. He will make you say who the customer is, what they already buy on that block, and whether the operator can survive the rent. Your practice work is a short list for one corridor you have walked. It stays inside the firm until someone else decides it is ready to use.

  4. 04

    How we communicate — the leasing brochure

    The brochure is the argument for the building. It is not a flyer with a floor plan dropped on it. Vanessa will show you how a page earns its place: the line of the neighborhood, the photographs, the plan, the tenant we want, and the thing we are willing to leave out.

    Your job is to learn the sequence. Story, then proof, then the ask. When you can describe a brochure that way, you are ready to talk about outreach. Not before.

  5. 05

    Mapping — walking, GrowthFactor, and CoStar

    We canvass. That means feet first. Walk the avenue and the side streets. Note what is open, what is papered, what went dark, who the neighbors are, and what the ground floor is actually doing at 11 a.m. and at 7 p.m. Food is a different business after dark. Jeff will make you see that.

    Then the screens, over someone’s shoulder. GrowthFactor tells you who visits, where they come from, and what else they go to. CoStar is the broker record: availabilities, comps, ownership, the listing other brokers see. LoopNet is the tenant-facing board. When we post an exclusive, CoStar speaks to brokers and LoopNet speaks to tenants. Same space, two audiences. You do not get a seat of your own. You learn the questions to ask while a teammate drives.

    In Hollywood, that CoStar session is with Eliot. Come having already read the Florida projects in the brand book.

  6. 06

    Email drip campaigns

    A drip is a short sequence to one kind of reader, with one reason for writing and one thing we want them to do. It is not a newsletter, and it is not a blast.

    You will outline one. Who it is for. The three notes, in order. What proof sits in each note. What the ask is on the last note. You will put that outline in Slack. You will not send it. You do not have an email seat, and a draft is not a campaign until the person who owns the relationship says it is.

  7. 07

    Life as an intern

    You are the subject. Shane produces. Jeff wants more of the team filming, so treat the shoot as a team job, not a favor from one person.

    October deliverable, due by October 31: the script, 45 to 60 seconds when read aloud at a normal pace. Time it. Structure it like this.

    • One or two sentences: who masonre is.
    • One or two sentences: what we do for a landlord and for a brand. Use a real example from the brand book if it helps, and keep it accurate.
    • One or two sentences: what you are here to learn between now and May, and what you will have done if the internship works.

    Read it to Jeff before you call it done. Shane will tell you what a camera cannot carry. We shoot after the words are settled, not as a way to find the words.

  8. 08

    Reading, so you learn the trends

    Once a week, on a day you are working, pick one story and send Jeff five lines in Slack. What happened. Who it affects. Whether it matters to a landlord, a brand, or both. What masonre would do with it, even if the answer is nothing.

    Read these:

    • The Real Deal, Commercial Observer, and Bisnow, for the deals and the markets.
    • Crain’s New York, for the city around the deals.
    • WWD and The Business of Fashion, for the brands that become tenants.
    • Eater NY and Grub Street, for restaurants, which are a leasing specialty of their own.

    If a better food or fashion source earns a place on the list, Jeff will add it. The habit matters more than the logo on the site.

Beside the eight: capital markets, with Elie

Leasing fills a building. Capital markets asks whether the building should be owned, and by whom. Elie will walk you through how masonre looks at a retail-driven asset: the street, the tenant mix, the rent, and whether the basis makes sense. You are not building a model. You are learning to see that a lease comp and a purchase thesis are the same block of sidewalk, read for two decisions.

Have the capital markets pages of the brand book read before you sit with him. One session is the February benchmark. More than one is better if his calendar allows.

Part II · Glossary

Words to keep rereading.

Retail brokerage, the way we talk. If a definition fights what Jeff tells you on a live deal, the live deal wins, and you update your notes.

The people

Landlord. The owner. We merchandise and lease their retail.
Tenant. The brand or operator who will occupy the space.
Exclusive. One firm is hired to represent the space or the assignment. We then have something to post and to stand behind.
Co-broker. Another broker in the deal. Know which side they are on before you repeat anything they say.
Credit. Shorthand for a tenant a landlord believes can pay. A famous name and a strong credit are not always the same thing.

The space

Frontage. The width on the street. In retail, width is often worth more than depth.
Inline. A store in the run of the block, not a corner and not a pad sitting alone.
Corner. Two frontages. Usually the space everyone asks for, and prices that way.
Flagship. The store that has to represent the brand, not just ring the register.
Second generation. A space another tenant already built. Kitchens, floors, and HVAC may be the opportunity.
White box / vanilla shell. Empty, finished enough to start from, without the last tenant’s business built in.
Dark. Closed. A dark store can still be under lease. Ask.
Divisible / demise. Cutting one space into more than one. 1140 Third is the example in our book.
Lower level. Basement or concourse. It leases when it has its own reason to exist, often its own entrance.
Retail condo. The retail owned separately from the apartments or the office above it. It can be leased, and it can be sold.

The rent

PSF. Per square foot, almost always per year in the way we quote rent.
Asking vs. taking. What the landlord publishes, and what actually gets signed. Believe comps of signed deals first.
NNN / triple net. Tenant pays rent plus taxes, insurance, and common-area costs. Know what “net” is net of.
Modified gross. A middle form. Some expenses sit with the landlord, some with the tenant. Read which ones.
CAM. Common area maintenance. The shared-cost bill. It changes the real rent.
TI. Tenant improvement allowance. Landlord money toward the buildout. It is part of the deal, not a gift beside it.
Free rent. Months of occupancy before rent starts. It is a concession with a cost.
Escalation. How the rent grows. A percentage a year, or bumps on a schedule.
Percentage rent. Extra rent as a share of sales, usually after a breakpoint. Common in retail, easy to misunderstand.
Term. How long the lease runs.
Possession. When the tenant can take the space.
Rent commencement. When rent starts. It is often not the possession date.
Glossary, continued

The deal

Canvass. Walking the market and calling on it. The job before the screen.
Availability. A space that can be leased. Confirm it. Listings go stale.
Comp. A comparable lease or sale. A comp without the concessions is half a fact.
LOI. Letter of intent. The business terms, before the lease is drafted.
Lease out. The deal is far enough that lawyers are papering it. Not the same as signed.
Use clause. What the tenant is allowed to operate. A bakery and a restaurant are not the same use.
Radius. A restriction on the tenant opening another store too close.
Exclusive use. Protection against a competing use in the same building.
Assignment. The tenant transfers the lease. Landlords guard this.
Good guy. A New York guaranty. The guarantor can get off the hook if the tenant pays through a noticed vacancy and gives the space back. Ask Jeff to show you one in a real lease.
Pro forma. The projection of rent, cost, and outcome. On a landlord job we often build one before the owner closes.
Merchandising. Deciding the mix: which uses, which sizes, which names, in which order.
Co-tenancy. Who sits next to whom. Fashion next to fashion is a strategy. So is a gym under apartments.

Food, because Jeff will ask

Venting. How cooking air leaves the building. No vent, no restaurant, unless the use does not need one.
Type 1 / Type 2. Type 1 handles grease from real cooking. Type 2 handles heat and steam. The menu decides which one.
Grease trap. The plumbing a cooking use often has to have. It is a deal issue, not a detail for later.
Second-generation restaurant. Someone already spent the money on the kitchen. Noda, in our book, is the lesson.

The market and the tools

Trade area. Where the customers actually come from. Not the same as the neighborhood’s nickname.
Corridor. The stretch of street that trades as one place. Madison, Lincoln Road, the Design District.
Daytime vs. residential. Office workers and people who sleep there are different customers. A block can have one and not the other.
CoStar. The system brokers use. Availabilities, comps, ownership. When we post an exclusive, this is the broker audience.
LoopNet. Where tenants and the public see listings. Same exclusive, different reader.
GrowthFactor. Visit data. Who comes, from where, and what else they frequent. We use it to test a story the walk suggested.
Drip. A short, ordered series of notes to one kind of reader, with one ask.
Cap rate. A price expressed as yield. You will hear it with Elie. You do not need to compute one this year. You need to know it is how buyers talk.
Basis. What an owner has in the deal. It drives what rent they can accept, and whether they will sell.
OM. Offering memorandum. The book for a building that is for sale. Cousin of the leasing brochure, different decision.
